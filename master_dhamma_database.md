## [brahmavihāra] [samatha]

### Source: interview/Ardmk 22-03-09.md

**Q:** What makes you think that upekkhā is related to the four jhānas?
**A:** The term upekkhā is spoken about in relation to the four jhānas. But upekkhā as a Brahmavihāra is a different one. Yes, it's different, I understand, but it's still similar. This is what the commentators say. It's not the same, but it's very similar. It would be a good question to ask what the difference is between them. Because in the suttas, it says you can practice the Brahmavihāra of loving-kindness, and so on, with vitakka-vicāra or without vitakka-vicāra. So it seems like all four jhānas can be accompanied by all four Brahmavihāras, including upekkhā. The commentary and the Visuddhimagga do not fully agree with this, however. They don't agree that upekkhā can be used in the first jhāna or second jhāna. I see no reason why it cannot be, and from personal experience, I think it can be used in all four jhānas.

Since I really love mettā, it's really the easiest object for me. I mean, if I were not engaged in pain at the moment of my death, there's a much greater chance I would be in mettā. I think it's unlikely I would remember any other object; I would likely remember mettā. That's why I think it would be good to start practicing the brahmavihāras together. I know, and especially upekkhā, I completely don't understand it. Just recently I gave a talk on the brahmavihāras for people, and I explained mettā, karuṇā, and muditā. I can explain them and how to practice them. But for upekkhā, I just told them, "No, silence." *Maṅku-bhūta*, you know, in this reflection the person becomes confused and punished. I didn't know what to say. It was something like, "What is upekkhā?" It was clear that upekkhā in jhāna and upekkhā here are different. I thought, yes, it's clearly different from the sutta. But what's different from a practical approach? I don't have much to say.

It is also good to balance the Brahmavihāras to some degree. There's not much harm in doing a bit more mettā than the others. But I think if a person or a tradition, like in the Mahāyāna tradition, focuses a lot on which one? The Mahāyāna tradition? On karuṇā, probably. So karuṇā is the main Brahmavihāra they emphasize. Interestingly, in the suttas, karuṇā is never mentioned alone. It always comes together with the other Brahmavihāras. But mettā is sometimes mentioned alone. I think there could be a reason for that, in that it is actually important to some degree to balance them out, because they have different emphases. If you do a lot of karuṇā especially, your mind is always about people who are suffering. It's also a kind of... okay, I'm not suffering, but then there are these people who are having these difficulties. It could be *māna* or a helper syndrome, where they think, "Oh, I have to help everyone. I know how to do it." You see this often with social jobs also, like social workers. It can be very difficult for them. That's why, if you balance it out with mettā or muditā... Muditā is actually the opposite direction, where you recollect the good things that other people have and you rejoice in that, basically, and get happiness from that. So it has a more upward direction. Mettā is maybe more or less on the same level, but karuṇā we could think of as a bit more downwards.

What's the advantage of being more on mettā rather than the other brahmavihāras? I think there is not so much. That's why I think mettā-predominance is not harmful, because the Buddha did mention mettā so much alone, whereas he never mentioned karuṇā or muditā alone. Upekkhā also comes around, but it's difficult to say which kind of upekkhā he meant. So if you wanted to see it in terms of direction, it would be like: mettā is maybe something on the same level—"may you also be well and happy." Karuṇā—"may you be free from suffering"—so they are there below me. Muditā—"oh, you're happy and successful"—maybe there. And upekkhā is like stepping back, distancing yourself from the person and looking at it in a more neutral way, where you are not so emotionally involved. In terms of directions, that's how it feels a little bit. They are all useful in their own right.

So that would be an idea: to start with the Brahmavihāras and go gradually and systematically through them, but not just with one, but really deeply with each one, where we will explore them in their full first jhāna, second jhāna, third jhāna, fourth jhāna. Yeah, arūpa... you know there is a sutta where you can gain enlightenment from mettā. I like it, if you reflect on it.
